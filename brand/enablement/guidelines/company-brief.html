Company brief

Everything the design system needs to say the right things about Speaking Genie: verified facts, the two commercial tracks, the full feature inventory, messaging that is safe to use, and the places where the attached sources contradict each other.

How to use this document

Every figure here is traceable to an attached source, named inline. Where sources disagree, the Investor Pitch Deck (June 2026) wins — it is the most recent and the most specific. Nothing here was inferred or rounded. If a number you need is not here, it was not in the sources: ask, do not invent.

The investor deck is a 14-page PDF with no text layer, so its figures were read from rendered page images. The embedded rasters are 800×451, which puts card body copy near the limit of legibility; every small-type figure below — client names, the cohort year, the year-one milestones, pricing bands and the funding split — was re-read from cropped upscales at 9× rather than from a page overview. Three errors were caught and corrected that way. If a further figure looks wrong to you, it is worth a second look at the source rather than trusting this document.

What the company is

Speaking Genie is an AI speaking-practice platform. A learner speaks out loud to an AI partner, gets corrected, and practises again — the product exists to close the distance between knowing a language and being able to speak it under pressure.

"The AI platform that gives non-native professionals the spoken confidence to pass interviews, close deals, and build global careers."
— Investor Deck, June 2026, cover. The current one-line positioning.

The central idea in every source is the Confidence Gap: proficiency is not the bottleneck, willingness to speak is. The company sells the practice reps that close it.

"Proficiency isn't the problem. Confidence is."
— Investor Deck p5

The insight that should shape the marketing

The end user is always the individual, but the cheapest way to reach them is through an organisation. The investor deck calls this the Blue Ocean Strategy: "Target: Individuals. Acquisition: B2B2C Partnerships." Rather than fighting a high-CAC ad war for direct users, centres and corporates deliver learners in bulk at near-zero acquisition cost.

For design this means the business-track material is not a side channel — it is the primary growth engine, and it deserves at least as much craft as the consumer feed.

Five features, and they map exactly onto the three things a centre or corporate buyer asks about: can I shape it to my syllabus, can I run my cohorts, and can I prove it worked.

Reading the feature list

Peer-to-peer call is a differentiator and it is underused. No competitor named in any source offers it — ChatGPT and Duolingo cannot, and human tutors are the thing it replaces at a fraction of the cost. It is the bridge between practising alone and speaking to a person, and the social work should lead with it more than it does.

Speaking-hours certificates are the commercial keystone. They are the artefact that makes practice legible to an employer, and they tie the B2C product to the B2B pitch: a corporate buyer is buying certified readiness, not app engagement.

ROI tracker earns the price. Of the five business features it is the only one that answers the renewal question. It should appear in every business-track deck, not just the product tour.

Why it wins

The competitive claim is precise and worth preserving exactly: Speaking Genie is the only curriculum-integrated speaking solution — it bridges generic AI and isolated learning apps.

AlternativeWhere it falls short

ChatGPT, Gemini		Instant, but vague context and no curriculum. Cannot grade against a syllabus.
Gamified apps
Duolingo, ELSA		Fun and habit-forming, but repetitive drills and little real speaking. Isolated from any institution.
Human tutorsEffective but $15/hr and unscalable. Also not judgment-free, which is the whole point of the product.

The technology claim

The deck is emphatic that AI is load-bearing: "AI is our Foundation, not a feature. If you remove AI, our product breaks." Three specifics support it:

  • Pedagogical assessment. Graded against Bloom's Taxonomy — assessing whether a learner is remembering or creating — with structured feedback instantly.
  • Curriculum ingestion. Ingests a centre's syllabus and generates role-plays specific to "Level 2, Chapter 4". Explicitly described as impossible for humans to do at scale.
  • Conversational velocity. An optimised low-latency TTS/STT pipeline targeting sub-second response, because lag breaks immersion.

The execution story is the most persuasive slide in the deck

Investor Deck p11 tells a four-week loop: shipped a conversational bot in week 1, heard "we need metrics, not just chat" from centres in week 2, shipped an automated grading dashboard by week 4, and signed two contracts the week after. It converts a churn risk into enterprise revenue and proves the team ships against feedback.

Nothing in the marketing material currently uses this. It should — it answers "why you" better than any feature list.

Do not say

  • Do not call TelePerformance a partner or client. It is pipeline.
  • Do not use the retired figures listed above, especially the $60B market and the unsourced confidence-gain percentages.
  • Do not promise a job outcome. The sources describe income potential and interview readiness, never guaranteed placement.
  • Do not describe the AI as a teacher or a replacement for one. Centres are partners; the product removes their grading workload, it does not remove them.
  • Do not name a customer without written permission beyond the five already public in the deck — Waghini, MVG, Jesr, HRG and The Pro English. Business decks use Jesr and HRG in "Also working with", and IMV Agency and Waghini through the approved testimonials.
  • Do not frame cost as savings. It is an investment per team member: 10 hours × 100 EGP = 1,000 EGP/month.

Source conflicts to resolve

1. Two different purples

The social posts sample at #4a0c79 flat and #1c0136 deep. All three decks and the company profile use #4C1D95 deep and #7C3AED primary — noticeably lighter and bluer.

Current handling: tokens carry the social purple as --purple-* because it came from produced artwork, and the deck purple resolves through the legacy --violet-* aliases. Both render. But two purples in one brand is a real inconsistency and only you can say which is correct.

2. Two different Latin typefaces

Every attached deck and the company profile load Plus Jakarta Sans. The design system was built on Poppins and Inter, taken from the enablement artifact. The investor deck's own headline face is heavier and more geometric than either.

Recommendation: standardise on Plus Jakarta Sans, since it is what every real produced document uses. This is a one-line change in tokens/fonts.css plus the two type tokens — say the word and I will make it.

3. A third accent colour

The company profile and Aladdin deck both use gold — #F59E0B and #FFD700 — for vision statements, story emphasis and the "magic" motif. The system currently defines only green (individuals) and orange (business). Gold is close enough to the orange to muddy the audience signal, so it is not in the tokens.

Open question: is gold a real third brand colour with a job, or a leftover from the Aladdin metaphor?

4. Client name spellings

  • "IMV Agency" (website testimonial) vs "MVG" (investor deck) — likely the same marketing agency.
  • "Wagihni" (website) vs "Waghini" (brief, deck, profile).

Current handling: testimonials use "IMV Agency" and "Waghini" as approved in the L&D deck. Confirm before external use.

5. Missing assets, still outstanding

  • Vector logo. The decks reference a hosted PNG; the system holds a raster extraction good to ~120px.
  • Arabic font files — Cairo is a substitution.
  • The thin-stroke line-art icon set used on the partner posts.
  • Photography library. Every deck currently pulls from Unsplash, which is fine for internal drafts and risky for paid media.
